\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{Chapter 5}

This chapter covers what the platform actually did in production during the second half of my internship, across the four lender tenants that were live. The numbers come out of the in-product analytics warehouse and the operational dashboards the team maintains. Anywhere a metric is tenant-sensitive (recovery percentages, mostly), the figures have been aggregated across tenants so that no individual lender can be picked out.

\section{Speed Dialer Throughput}
Before the speed dialer landed, a typical agent on the platform was handling somewhere between forty and fifty calls in an eight-hour shift. After it rolled out, the same agents started doing eighty to ninety-five in the same shift. The mean shifted from about 46 to about 87 per shift, and the variance between agents collapsed pretty sharply -- because the old variance had mostly been about individual quirks in case lookup and dialling, both of which the speed dialer now does for everybody, the same way.

Per-call handle time fell too. Measured end to end -- from the moment an agent picks up the case to the moment they submit a disposition -- the average was 8.3 minutes before the rollout. After: 4.6 minutes. Pulling apart where the 3.7 minutes went: almost all of it comes out of killing the inter-call manual work (hunting for the next case in the spreadsheet, reading the history), not out of anything that happens during the call itself. The in-call portion only dropped by about thirty seconds. The dead time between calls collapsed from about three minutes to roughly ten seconds.

The single cleanest way to see the combined effect of all of this -- multi-channel orchestration, after-hours coverage, faster handling per call -- is the day-by-day recovery curve over a cycle. Figure~\ref{fig:recovery-curve} plots that, for the same cohort of allocated cases, across the thirty-day collection cycle, under the agency-only baseline and again under the platform. The two curves diverge most aggressively in the first five days, where the combination of AI calling, WhatsApp reminders and speed-dialed human follow-up does most of its work. After the first week the curves continue to widen, but more gently.

\section{WhatsApp Sender Pool Behaviour}
The WhatsApp sender pool is the most operationally sensitive part of the platform. Bans are not rare events -- a sender that has been pushed hard for a few weeks will eventually get flagged and need either a cool-down period or a replacement. Table~\ref{tab:wa-bans} shows the ban distribution we actually observed across the five months that the SIM-based WhatsApp workers were running.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{WhatsApp sender ban distribution.}
\label{tab:wa-bans}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{l c c}
\toprule
\textbf{Sender age (days)} & \textbf{Ban probability per day} & \textbf{Median messages before ban} \\
\midrule
0--7      & 8\%   & 230 \\
8--30     & 3\%   & 870 \\
31--90    & 1.5\% & 1{,}640 \\
91--180   & 0.9\% & 2{,}340 \\
180+      & 0.4\% & 3{,}900 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The pattern is roughly what you'd expect: fresh numbers get flagged much more easily than aged ones. The best-practice we ended up codifying from the data is a graduated warm-up -- start a new number in the pool with a low daily cap of around thirty messages for the first week, gradually raise the cap over the next three weeks, and only let the number hit its full cap of one hundred and fifty after a full month of warm-up.

The other change that made a visible difference was the block-detection logic. Before we added explicit error-substring matching and soft-failure handling, the median time between a ban actually happening and somebody on the team noticing was around eighteen hours. Now the orchestrator pulls a banned sender out of rotation within thirty seconds of the first banned-class error -- and reassigns the orphan contacts in the same operation.

\section{AI Voice Agent Performance}
There are three things we measure the AI voice agent on: latency, contact rate, and disposition accuracy. On latency, the budget the internal voice AI platform targets held up for the most part. The median turn-end-to-agent-start latency, on the warmer half of the turn distribution, was 690 milliseconds. The tail is the problem. The 95th percentile is 1.4 seconds and the 99th crosses 2.2. Figure~\ref{fig:voice-latency} plots all three percentile bars against the 800--900 ms band -- the band at which human listeners start to feel something is slightly off. The median sits comfortably below it, the p95 crosses it, and the p99 is roughly three times the perceptual threshold.

The tail is mostly ASR cold starts. The next release cycle has work scheduled on that.

Disposition accuracy is the share of AI-call outcomes that QA agrees with when they sample and review. The QA portal pulls a random five percent of all AI calls and routes each one to a reviewer. The reviewer then relistens to the recording, reads the LLM-generated transcript, and either confirms the disposition or overrides it. Across about six thousand audited calls so far, the QA-confirmed accuracy is 91 percent. The single most common disagreement type is the AI agent classifying something as a Promise to Pay when the customer was actually being evasive about a specific date. It's a known training-data gap and an upstream prompt change is in flight to address it.

\section{Failure Modes and Stakeholder Feedback}
No results chapter is complete without saying honestly what went wrong. There were three production failures during the internship that had real impact, and they're worth recording.

The first was a search-index connection leak that exhausted file descriptors after a few days of running. The fix was a pooled connection manager with an idle-eviction policy on it. The second was a race condition between the voice platform's terminal callback and the defensive status-poll job we schedule alongside every call. The fix there was to cancel the in-flight poll job from inside the callback handler, so the existing already-processed guard could absorb the laggard if the poll job had already started. The third was a timestamp-format change in the voice platform's outbound payload that broke our strict parser on the workflow-poll path. We replaced the strict parser with a more tolerant one and wired up an alert on any schema break in the inbound payload. Each of the three turned into a runbook entry, a test case, and a configuration change so they don't recur silently.

On qualitative feedback, the people who actually use the platform have been pretty consistent. Floor managers at the agencies told us the speed dialer cut agent ramp-up from four weeks down to under two. Lender-side operations teams told us they valued the in-product analytics most -- it was the first time they could have a bucket-level or agent-level conversation with the agency without an argument about whose numbers were right. The compliance side valued the call-frequency guard, because it removed an entire category of audit finding from the lender's quarterly review. The platform today is moving every metric the project set out to move.
