\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{Chapter 5}

This chapter covers what the platform actually did in production during the second half of my internship, across the four lender tenants that were live. The numbers come out of the in-product analytics warehouse and the operational dashboards the team maintains. Anywhere a metric is tenant-sensitive (recovery percentages, mostly), the figures have been aggregated across tenants so that no individual lender can be picked out.

\section{Speed Dialer Throughput}
Before the speed dialer existed, a typical agent handled between forty and fifty calls in an eight-hour shift. After it rolled out, the same agents were doing eighty to ninety-five calls in the same shift. The mean moved from about 46 to about 87 calls per shift, and the variance between agents collapsed sharply -- most of the prior variance had been about individual habits around case-lookup and dialling, both of which the speed dialer now does for everyone.

Per-call handle time fell too. Before the speed dialer the average handle time -- measured from the agent picking up a case to submitting a disposition -- was 8.3 minutes. After the rollout it was 4.6 minutes. Pulling that 3.7-minute saving apart, almost all of it comes from killing the inter-call manual work (finding the next case, skimming the history) rather than from anything happening during the call itself. The in-call duration only fell by about thirty seconds. The gap between calls dropped from three minutes to roughly ten seconds.

The cleanest single picture of the combined effect of all this -- multi-channel orchestration, after-hours coverage and faster handling -- is the day-by-day recovery curve over a cycle. Figure~\ref{fig:recovery-curve} plots, for the same cohort of allocated cases, the share recovered by the end of each day of the thirty-day collection cycle, under the agency-only baseline and under the platform. The two curves diverge most sharply in the first five days, which is where the combination of AI calling, WhatsApp reminders and speed-dialed human follow-up does most of its work. After the first week the two curves keep widening, but more slowly.

\section{WhatsApp Sender Pool Behaviour}
The WhatsApp sender pool is the most operationally sensitive part of the platform. Bans are not rare events -- a sender that has been pushed hard for a few weeks will eventually get flagged and need either a cool-down period or a replacement. Table~\ref{tab:wa-bans} shows the ban distribution we actually observed across the five months that the SIM-based WhatsApp workers were running.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{WhatsApp sender ban distribution.}
\label{tab:wa-bans}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{l c c}
\toprule
\textbf{Sender age (days)} & \textbf{Ban probability per day} & \textbf{Median messages before ban} \\
\midrule
0--7      & 8\%   & 230 \\
8--30     & 3\%   & 870 \\
31--90    & 1.5\% & 1{,}640 \\
91--180   & 0.9\% & 2{,}340 \\
180+      & 0.4\% & 3{,}900 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The pattern is the obvious one: freshly registered numbers get flagged much more readily than aged numbers. The best-practice we settled on from this data is to introduce new numbers into the pool with a low daily cap (around thirty messages a day) for the first week, raise the cap gradually over the next three weeks, and only let the number hit its full cap of one hundred and fifty after a full month of warm-up.

The other improvement that made a visible dent was the block-detection logic. Before we added explicit error-substring matching and soft-failure handling, the median time between a ban and someone noticing was about eighteen hours. After the changes, the orchestrator removes a banned sender from rotation within thirty seconds of the first banned-class error and reassigns the orphan contacts in the same step.

\section{AI Voice Agent Performance}
The AI voice agent gets measured on three things: latency, contact rate and disposition accuracy. The latency budget the internal voice AI platform targets held up in production for the most part. The median turn-end-to-agent-start latency, measured on the warmer half of the turn distribution, was 690 milliseconds. The tail, however, is much worse: the 95th percentile is 1.4 seconds and the 99th crosses 2.2. Figure~\ref{fig:voice-latency} plots the three percentile bars against the 800--900 ms band at which human listeners start to feel something is off. The median sits comfortably below the band, the p95 crosses it, and the p99 is roughly three times the perceptual threshold.

The tail is dominated by ASR cold starts; the next release cycle has work scheduled on it.

Disposition accuracy is the share of AI-call outcomes that get classified correctly when QA samples and reviews them. The QA portal pulls a random five percent of all AI calls and hands each to a reviewer, who relistens to the recording, reads the LLM-generated transcript and either confirms or overrides the disposition. Across roughly six thousand audited calls, the QA-confirmed accuracy is 91 percent. The most common disagreement type is the AI agent calling something a Promise to Pay when the customer was actually evasive about a specific date. This is a known training-data gap and is being fixed by an upstream prompt change.

\section{Failure Modes and Stakeholder Feedback}
A results chapter is incomplete without an honest record of what broke in production. The three most consequential failures were the following. First, a search-index connection leak that exhausted file descriptors after a few days of running -- fixed by a pooled connection manager with an idle-eviction policy. Second, a race between the voice platform's terminal callback and the defensive status-poll job. The fix was to cancel the in-flight poll job inside the callback handler so the existing already-processed guard could absorb the laggard. Third, a timestamp-format change in the voice platform's payload that broke the orchestrator's strict parser on the workflow-poll path. The fix was a more tolerant parser plus an alert on any schema break in the inbound payload. Each of these turned into a runbook entry, a test case and a configuration change to prevent it from recurring.

The qualitative feedback from the people who actually use the platform has been uniformly positive. Agency floor managers told us the speed dialer collapsed the typical agent ramp-up time from four weeks to under two. Lender-side operations teams valued the in-product analytics: for the first time they could have a bucket-level and agent-level conversation with the agency without arguing about the numbers. The compliance team valued the call-frequency guard, because it removed an entire category of audit findings from the lender's quarterly review. As it stands today the platform has moved every metric the project set out to improve.
