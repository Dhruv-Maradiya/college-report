\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{Chapter 5}

This chapter reports the quantitative and qualitative results obtained from the deployment of the AI-Based Collections Platform across the four lender tenants that were active in production during the latter half of the internship. The numbers are pulled from the in-product ClickHouse warehouse and from the Grafana dashboards that the engineering team maintains. Where the metric is sensitive (for example, lender-specific recovery percentages), the figures have been aggregated across tenants so that no individual tenant can be re-identified.

\section{Speed Dialer Throughput}
Before the speed dialer was introduced, a typical agent on the platform handled between forty and fifty calls in an eight-hour shift. After the speed dialer was rolled out, the same agents began handling between eighty and ninety-five calls in the same shift. The mean rose from about 46 to about 87 calls per shift, and the agent-to-agent variance collapsed sharply because the bulk of the prior variance came from individual habits around case-lookup and dialling -- both of which the speed dialer fully automates.

The handle time per call also fell substantially. Before the speed dialer, the average handle time -- defined as the time from the agent picking up a case to the disposition being submitted -- was 8.3 minutes. After the rollout it fell to 4.6 minutes. The decomposition of that 3.7-minute saving suggests that the bulk of the saving comes from the elimination of inter-call manual work (finding the next case, reading the customer history): the in-call duration itself fell by only about thirty seconds, while the inter-call interval collapsed from an average of three minutes to about ten seconds.

The most direct way of expressing the cumulative effect of these changes -- multi-channel orchestration, after-hours coverage and faster handling -- is the day-by-day recovery curve over a cycle. Figure~\ref{fig:recovery-curve} plots, for the same cohort of allocated cases, the percentage that has been recovered by the end of each day of the thirty-day collection cycle, under the agency-only baseline and under the platform. The two curves diverge most aggressively in the first five days, which is where the platform's combination of AI calling, WhatsApp reminders and speed-dialed human follow-up does most of its work; after the first week the two curves continue to widen but at a slower pace.

\section{WhatsApp Sender Pool Behaviour}
The WhatsApp sender pool is the most operationally sensitive sub-system of the platform. Bans are routine; a sender that has been heavily used for a few weeks will eventually be flagged and require either a cool-down period or replacement. Table~\ref{tab:wa-bans} reports the empirical ban distribution observed across the five-month period in which the SIM-based WhatsApp workers were running.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{WhatsApp sender ban distribution.}
\label{tab:wa-bans}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{l c c}
\toprule
\textbf{Sender age (days)} & \textbf{Ban probability per day} & \textbf{Median messages before ban} \\
\midrule
0--7      & 8\%   & 230 \\
8--30     & 3\%   & 870 \\
31--90    & 1.5\% & 1{,}640 \\
91--180   & 0.9\% & 2{,}340 \\
180+      & 0.4\% & 3{,}900 \\
\bottomrule

The pattern is intuitive: freshly registered numbers are flagged much more readily than aged numbers. Best practice that emerged from the data is to introduce new numbers into the pool with a low daily cap (typically thirty messages per day) for the first week, gradually raise the cap over the next three weeks, and only bring the number to its full cap of one hundred and fifty after a month of warm-up.

The block-detection logic was the second improvement that made a measurable difference. Before the explicit error-substring matching and the soft-failure handling were added, the median time from a ban to the operator noticing it was about eighteen hours. After the changes, the orchestrator removes a banned sender from rotation within thirty seconds of the first banned-class error and reassigns the orphan contacts immediately.

\section{AI Voice Agent Performance}
The AI voice agent's performance is measured along three dimensions: latency, contact rate and disposition accuracy. The latency budget that Bolna advertises was largely upheld in production: the median turn-end-to-agent-start latency, measured on the warmer half of the turn distribution, was 690 milliseconds. The slower tail, however, is materially worse -- the 95th-percentile latency is 1.4~seconds and the 99th-percentile crosses 2.2~seconds. This tail is dominated by ASR cold starts and is being investigated as part of the next release cycle.

The disposition accuracy is the share of AI-call outcomes that, when randomly sampled and reviewed by the QA team, are classified correctly. The QA portal samples five percent of all AI calls and routes them to a QA reviewer who relistens to the recording, reads the LLM-generated transcript and either confirms or overrides the disposition. The QA-confirmed accuracy across roughly six thousand audited calls is 91~percent. The most common disagreement type is the AI agent classifying an outcome as a Promise to Pay when the customer was actually evasive about a specific date, which is a known training-data gap and is being addressed by an upstream prompt change.

\section{Failure Modes and Stakeholder Feedback}
A complete result analysis is incomplete without a record of the failure modes encountered in production. The three most consequential were a Solr connection leak on ECS that exhausted file descriptors over a few days of running and was fixed by a pooled connection manager with an idle-eviction policy; a webhook race between Bolna's terminal webhook and a defensive status-poll job, fixed by cancelling the in-flight poll job inside the webhook handler so that the existing already-processed guard absorbs the laggard; and a Bolna log-timestamp format change that broke the orchestrator's strict parser on the workflow-poll path, fixed by a tolerant parser plus a Sentry alert on any schema break in Bolna's payload. Each of these resulted in a runbook entry, a test case and a configuration change that prevents recurrence.

The qualitative feedback from stakeholders involved in the deployment was uniformly positive. Agency floor managers reported that the speed dialer collapsed agent ramp-up time from four weeks to under two. Lender-side operations teams valued the in-product analytics, which made the bucket-level and agent-level conversation with the agency tractable for the first time. The compliance team valued the call-frequency guard because it removed an entire category of audit findings from the lender's quarterly review. The platform in its current state has been a material step forward against every metric the project set out to improve.
