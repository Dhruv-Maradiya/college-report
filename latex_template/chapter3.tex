\chapter{Proposed Methodology and Implementation}
\label{Chapter 3}

This is the long chapter. Three things I want to get through here, in this order. How the team actually ships at Ignosis. The platform at a thousand-foot view. Then, going subsystem by subsystem, every piece I touched. I am keeping the chapter feature-driven on purpose -- the platform's brain is a tenant-configurable workflow engine, and almost everything else (the campaign module, the AI voice integration, the WhatsApp service, the speed dialer) is either feeding it work or consuming the actions it produces.

\section{Development Methodology}
Engineering at Ignosis is plan-first, ship-small, very review-heavy. I didn't appreciate what that meant in my first week. I wrote what I thought was a small refactor of the tenant-context propagation across async boundaries (my second ticket), pushed it to a feature branch, and got back nine comments, two of which said ``do you want to write a one-pager first and we'll talk through it''. That was the lesson. Every task starts as a short markdown plan attached to the ticket. The engineering lead reads it. You go back and forth in the ticket for a day, maybe two. Only after that does any code get written.

The rest of the workflow is conventional. A feature branch goes up from develop. Conventional commits are mandatory and the CI enforces them. PRs to develop are rebased, not squashed, on purpose, so the per-commit decision trail survives the merge -- this surprised me at first, but it's saved me at least twice when I needed to figure out why some line of code existed. The release branch is on semantic versioning and is cut off main. Tests sit next to the code they exercise. There is a unit-test framework on the backend and a parallel test runner on the frontend. CI fails any build where the static-analysis tool flags a new bug, a new code smell above medium severity, or a new vulnerability of any severity, and there is a fifty-percent coverage floor on changed lines. (I learned about the coverage floor the way you'd expect, which was by failing a PR on a Friday evening.)

\section{High-Level Architecture}
At the highest level the platform is a layered web application. Three client surfaces sit at the front. The agent dashboard, which is a browser app. A desktop client which is where the WhatsApp workers actually run. And a small Android SMS sender app. Behind these three sit the orchestrator backend and a much smaller automation service. The data layer is a mix -- a relational database for cases, campaigns and jobs; a search index for free-text queries; an analytics event store for reporting; and an in-memory queue with locks for transient state. Beneath the data layer the platform reaches out to three families of external services. There is the internal voice AI platform paired with a telephony gateway, which is what actually places calls. There is the WhatsApp protocol, talked to via a third-party multi-device library. And there is the cellular SMS network, accessed through whatever the Android phone has built in. All of that fits on one diagram, in Figure~\ref{fig:arch}.

It took me longer than I'd like to admit to grasp how this all fits together. I spent most of my first two weeks just reading the orchestrator and chasing where things called what. The choice of a single multi-tenant monolith for the backend instead of a fleet of microservices was made by the team well before I joined, and it was deliberate. The team is small (six engineers, growing slowly), the deployment volume is modest, and the operational cost of running microservices wasn't worth what you'd get back. The boundaries inside the orchestrator are kept clean enough that, if the volume ever justifies a split, the rewrite would not be a rewrite.

\section{Case Management}
\label{sec:case-mgmt}
Everywhere in the platform the unit of work is the {\em case}. A case maps to one delinquent loan account that the tenant has been allocated for the current cycle. Each one carries a fair bit -- the customer's contact details, the loan account number, the principal and the EMI, the days past due, the bucket it sits in, the agent or AI track it has been routed to, the workflow node it is currently parked on, and a journal of every interaction that has ever happened against it.

The first real frontend ticket I picked up was the case listing screen. It's a server-side-paginated table of whatever cases are assigned to the agent who's logged in. The columns are the usual ones: customer name, loan account number, DPD, overdue amount, last contact attempt, current status. Filtering is evaluated on the backend. Free-text search hits a dedicated index. The case detail screen is the more interesting one, because it brings together the contact info, the loan and payment history, every prior call transcript and its outcome, the WhatsApp and SMS threads, the AA-derived signals where we have them, and the case's current workflow position. The very first cut of that page did one big sequential database fan-out and took about six seconds to render for any tenant with a busy timeline. The PR review I got from Mr.~Shah came back as three words: ``progressive loading please''. So the page now loads the core information first, and the heavier interaction panels stream in behind it in parallel. That one change brought the page back to under a second on the same tenant.

\section{The Workflow Engine}
\label{sec:workflow}
The biggest source of variation across tenants is the collection policy itself. A private bank tends to want a calm tele-calling sequence with WhatsApp reminders dropped in between. An NBFC wants the opposite -- an aggressive AI voice campaign for the first ninety-six hours, and a human only if the AI has given up. If we hard-coded any of this into the orchestrator, every time a tenant tweaked its policy we would be looking at a code change and a deployment. (The first cut of the orchestrator actually worked that way, and it was painful enough that the team rewrote it before I joined.)

The current platform stores the policy as data instead. Each tenant has one or more workflows, where a workflow is a directed graph. Nodes are concrete actions -- initiate an AI call, send a WhatsApp reminder, push a payment link, schedule an agent callback, wait for payment, escalate to a human. Transitions are simple boolean conditions over the case's current variables. A typical transition reads as ``advance to the wait-for-payment node if the customer promised to pay in the next three days, otherwise fall through to the next branch''. The engine, for each tick: looks at the current node, runs its action (synchronously if it's cheap, asynchronously via a scheduled job otherwise), writes the outcome back into the case's variable context, walks the outgoing transitions in declaration order, and parks the case on whichever node matches first. If nothing matches, the case lands in an error state for QA to look at. That last bit is in there because in the first month of one of the tenants we had a few badly-authored workflows that silently looped on themselves. Visible failure beats silent looping every time.

Anything that touches an external system runs as an asynchronous job. The engine writes a row into a database-backed queue. A scheduler polls for due jobs, takes a row-level lock with a short time-to-live, and runs the relevant executor. Why a database queue and not, say, an in-memory one? One reason: visibility. If a campaign is misbehaving at 3 AM, the on-call engineer can query the job table directly and see exactly where each individual case is stuck. The trade-off is throughput, but at the campaign volumes we run in production, throughput has not yet been the limiting factor.

\section{The Campaign Module}
\label{sec:campaign}
The campaign is the platform's unit of bulk work. The shorthand definition is ``run this workflow against this batch of cases, within this calling window''. The creation form asks for a campaign name, a priority, a target collection amount, an allocation rule (round-robin, weighted, or manual) and the calling window. The CSV upload field will take files of up to fifty megabytes. Under the hood, the CSV-processing service parses the file and runs each row through a short rule set: non-blank phone in international format, non-blank name, positive due amount, a loan account number that looks valid, a bucket the platform recognises, a DPD value inside the expected range. The output is a preview with per-row diagnostics. The campaign manager reads through the preview, fixes anything that's obviously off, and then submits.

Two things about this flow that aren't obvious from the description. First, the validator rejects rows individually but never rejects the whole file. A 4,000-row CSV almost always has a handful of broken rows in it -- a phone number with the country code missing, a bucket label that doesn't line up with the DPD, that kind of thing -- and stopping the entire upload on those would make the tool unusable. So the preview flags bad rows, those rows are dropped silently at submission, and a separate downloadable report tells the manager what got skipped. Second: for the first two weeks of one of the tenant onboardings, the CSVs they uploaded had a BOM marker at the start of the first header. The parser was breaking silently. Fixed now, the parser handles BOMs, but it's the reason the preview today also includes a ``did we recognise your columns'' line.

Once the campaign is submitted, the backend persists the campaign object and one case record for every valid row, and the campaign drops into a scheduled (or, if the calling window is already open, ongoing) state. From the moment it goes ongoing, the scheduler picks it up on the next tick and starts queueing workflow-advancement jobs for its rows. After that the workflow engine drives every case forward independently. The campaign itself keeps a running tally of progress -- cases by state, money collected so far, calls attempted, calls connected -- and streams those numbers to the dashboard. A campaign can be paused, resumed, or marked complete at any point. The largest campaign we have run through the platform so far had 41,200 cases in it. The scheduler took about seven minutes to enqueue all the initial advancement jobs. That run, and the question of whether the queue would keep up, was the first time I really had to think about platform throughput.

\section{AI Voice Calling}
\label{sec:voice}
This is the headline feature of the platform. From an architectural standpoint, an AI call is just one of the action types the workflow engine knows how to run. When the engine reaches an AI call node for a case, it schedules a job. The executor reads the tenant's configured voice-agent identifier, pulls the next phone number from the tenant's pool of caller-line identifiers, builds the call request from the case context (customer name, due amount, DPD, AA signals if available), and hands the request off to the internal voice AI platform. From here on the voice platform owns the call until it terminates.

The voice platform places the call through the telephony gateway, runs the conversation under the tenant's prompt template, and fires a callback to the orchestrator when the call ends. The callback carries the disposition, a structured extraction of variables (promise-to-pay date, dispute reason, callback request, language detected), a recording URL and the per-turn latency. The orchestrator parses it, persists the outcome onto the case, cancels any defensive status-poll job that was scheduled as a safety net, emits metrics for the call, and re-enters the workflow engine for that case so the workflow advances on the same callback request.

The first time I listened to one of these recordings end to end, I was sitting in the team area on a Tuesday and a customer in Lucknow had agreed to pay on the 18th. The AI never raised its voice once. I think that's when I actually understood why the team was so invested in this subsystem.

The speed dialer is the user-facing twin of the AI calling path. While an agent is working on a campaign, the dashboard shows the active case in a panel. The agent talks to the customer, fills out a short disposition form and hits submit. The instant the disposition lands at the backend, the workflow engine advances the case, the case enters a terminal-for-this-agent state, and the next pending case is pushed to the dashboard over the streaming channel. From the agent's seat, the next customer is already on screen as the previous form is closing. The agency floor manager I demoed this to in March told me her agents had stopped using sticky notes for the next number to dial. I think that's the cleanest user feedback any of my work has gotten this year.

\section{WhatsApp Automation}
\label{sec:whatsapp}
WhatsApp automation lives in its own automation toolkit. We kept it deliberately apart from the orchestrator for operational reasons. WhatsApp sessions are long-lived stateful connections. The first pairing needs an actual phone scanning a QR code. The sessions also have to survive infrastructure restarts. None of those properties fit comfortably inside a stateless backend service. The toolkit is built on top of a third-party multi-device library that lets us drive a pool of consumer SIMs in software. Picking that library over Meta's official business API was an easy call -- the business API drags template approvals over days, charges per message, and (empirically) customers respond much better to a message from a real consumer number than to one from a branded business account.

The very first cut of this subsystem ran on a single phone. It was a CLI tool that took a JSON file of contacts and shipped them out one at a time. Worked fine for ten messages. Fell over at a hundred. The second version came two weeks later -- a service driving a pool of phones with a shared queue. The third version (which is what runs in production now) added preferred-sender routing, per-sender daily caps, a warm-up schedule, and a proper block-recovery path. I'd love to claim the three versions were planned. They weren't. The first was a prototype I rushed. The second got rewritten on a Sunday because I'd promised myself the weekend off and then could not stop thinking about a bug. The third was the only one that actually had a design document, written after Mr.~Shah caught me sketching the second rewrite on a whiteboard and pointed out, gently, that this was the kind of thing we wrote down.

Any WhatsApp campaign is bound to a pool of five to fifteen sender phones. At the start of the campaign, the scheduler does a proportional allocation of contacts across the pool, with each sender capped at its own daily limit. The shared contact queue carries a {\em preferred sender} field so contacts can be soft-routed to a specific phone -- usually the one that already has an active thread with that customer. If the preferred sender happens to be offline or banned, the contact falls back to whichever worker pulls it next. Net effect: when individual senders die mid-cycle, the campaign keeps moving. They do die. We lost three of them in one afternoon during a campaign in mid-March, and the system reassigned the orphans cleanly. I was holding my breath the whole time.

A real chunk of the engineering on this subsystem goes into staying ahead of spam-detection heuristics. Each sender has a daily cap. The inter-message delay is jittered by forty percent around a per-sender base interval. Batches are three to twelve messages followed by a short break; every fifth batch triggers a longer break. The workers honour an active-hours window. Every outgoing message body is varied -- inline alternates plus a zero-width space inserted at a random character position. That last trick I picked up from a Stack Overflow thread I'd only half-read on a Tuesday morning. Block detection is reactive but fast: the moment the protocol library signals a ban, the sender drops out of rotation, the suspected ban gets logged, and the orphaned contacts on the shared queue are reassigned to the remaining workers. Connection-class transient errors are handled separately, with exponential backoff. From the workflow's point of view: the WhatsApp reminder node posts a job to the automation service, the service does the send, waits for the protocol-level acknowledgement, and hands the delivery outcome back to the orchestrator -- the workflow then advances based on that outcome.

\section{SMS via Android}
The SMS fallback channel is a small Android app. It runs on a phone that has an active consumer SIM in it. Sending and reading messages go through the device's built-in SMS interface. Outbound sends respect a per-message delay, a batch size and a per-batch pause -- all of that exists to keep the SIM operator from getting suspicious and flagging the device. The specific numbers we settled on (twelve seconds between messages, batches of twenty, a forty-five-second gap between batches) came out of about two weeks of trial-and-error on a single Redmi handset we keep in the office for exactly this. An evening background task reads any new SMS messages off the on-device provider, bundles them up, and uploads them to the orchestrator. That way the agent sees every reply alongside the rest of the case timeline. As far as the workflow engine is concerned, an SMS reminder node is structurally identical to a WhatsApp reminder node -- produces an outcome, outcome drives the next transition.

\section{The Desktop Application}
The desktop application is the thing that hands the WhatsApp workers to the engineering team and to a small set of agency-side operators. On launch, it boots the embedded automation server, serves the dashboard locally, and opens a window pointing at it. It has two jobs. One: actually running WhatsApp campaigns from the operator's own machine with the SIMs paired locally. Two: scraping the chat history of those paired phones at the end of each day, so the case dashboard can show every WhatsApp conversation right next to the call history.

The first build had the local server's port hard-coded. That happened to work on three of the four machines we tested. On the fourth machine the port clashed with another running process, and the app surfaced a one-line cryptic error that did not actually say anything about a port conflict. I spent the better part of an afternoon on a video call with the operator trying to figure out what had gone wrong. The free-port-finding logic that's in the current build should obviously have been in the first one.

\section{Data Analysis on Customer Records}
Very early on (I think it was week four), I ran a one-off analysis on a sample of about forty thousand customer records exported from one of the lender tenants. The file arrived as a 220 MB CSV with somewhere around twenty-six columns, and the data quality was rough -- there were several obvious issues I had to clean up before anything sensible could come out of it. I spent two days on it. Three patterns showed up, all with real implications for how we should be running campaigns.

The first one: customers who had paid on the first attempt in any of the previous three cycles had a ninety-percent probability of paying on the first attempt in the next cycle as well. That cohort doesn't need a full call campaign at all -- a single SMS reminder is enough, which saves both the agency's time and the lender's money. The second one: customers whose detected salary credit landed within the first three days of the month showed a noticeably higher response rate to outreach attempted on the fourth or fifth day. The effect wasn't huge, but it was remarkably consistent across tenants. The third was a tail: customers with ticket sizes under a thousand rupees and recovery probabilities below ten percent. For that segment a single SMS is the only touch that makes any economic sense. All three findings went into the default workflow templates the platform now ships with for new tenants.

\section{Summary of Contributions}
To pull all of the above together. The pieces I contributed to the platform are these. On the dashboard side: the case listing screen and the case detail screen. On the campaign side: the bulk campaign module end to end, both the creation form and the CSV upload pipeline behind it. The speed dialer, together with the wiring that integrates AI voice calling into the workflow engine. The WhatsApp automation -- session management, the sender pool scheduler, the anti-ban heuristics, and the multi-device rotation, block detection and message variation that sit underneath. The desktop application. The Android SMS sender app together with its background sync. The voice-platform callback integration on the workflow path. And finally the one-off analysis of customer records that fed into the default workflow templates. Each of these either feeds the workflow engine, consumes what the engine produces, or shaves time off the human agent's interaction with cases the workflow has already prioritised. Every number that appears in Chapter~\ref{Chapter 5} ties back to this workflow-centric structure.
