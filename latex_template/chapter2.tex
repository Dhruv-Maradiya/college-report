\section{Previous Approaches to Solve the Problem}

\subsection{Existing Collection-Management Products}
The space of commercial collection-management products in India is dominated by a small handful of vendors whose offerings predate the recent wave of conversational AI: CreditMantri, Spocto, Cred and a few internal tools built by the larger private-sector banks make up the majority of the visible deployments. The architecture of these products tends to follow a familiar shape -- a case management database, a dialer integration, some reporting -- but the actual conversational layer is almost always human-driven. WhatsApp, where it is used, is plugged in through the official Cloud API published by Meta and is therefore subject to template-approval delays, throughput throttling and per-message pricing that grows linearly with volume. The academic literature on collection software per se is thin -- most of the deep work happens inside banks and is not published -- but the adjacent literature on contact-centre optimisation and on credit-scoring models is large. Verma, Mathur and Singh~\cite{vermaml} survey machine learning techniques applied to behavioural credit data, several of which are directly applicable to predicting which customer is most likely to respond to which channel. Wang and Zhang~\cite{contactcenter} review predictive dialers and the queue-management algorithms that underpin them. None of this prior work, however, addresses the combination of AI voice calling, AA-data-driven prioritisation and SIM-based WhatsApp automation that motivates the present project.

\subsection{Conversational Voice Agents}
The maturity of conversational voice agents is the single biggest technology shift that makes an AI-driven collection platform possible. Modern voice agents are built on a pipeline of automatic speech recognition (ASR), a large language model (LLM) for dialogue generation and a text-to-speech (TTS) synthesiser, with each component reaching real-time latency budgets that did not exist three years ago. The firm's internal voice AI platform~\cite{bolna} is the conversational voice stack that Ignosis uses in production; it chains a low-latency ASR, a fine-tuned LLM and a TTS engine that can emit Indian English with regional-accent variation. The latency budget the platform targets is roughly 700~ms from the customer finishing their utterance to the agent beginning to speak, which is below the 800--900~ms threshold at which human listeners begin to detect awkwardness. Open-source baselines such as Pipecat~\cite{pipecat} and LiveKit Agents~\cite{livekit} were briefly evaluated before the internal platform was confirmed for production; the decision matrix turned out to be dominated by Indian-English ASR accuracy on rural and semi-urban customers and the maturity of the post-call extraction pipeline. \subsection{The WhatsApp Multi-Device Protocol}
WhatsApp's multi-device protocol allows up to four secondary devices to share end-to-end-encrypted access to a primary phone's account without the phone needing to be online. The official integration path for businesses is the WhatsApp Cloud API, which exposes a REST interface and enforces a template-message policy for the first message in any conversation. For a collection use case the Cloud API is operationally restrictive: every outbound template has to be approved by Meta, the per-message pricing makes the channel uneconomic for low-ticket-size cases, and customers respond more readily to messages that come from real consumer-grade WhatsApp numbers than from obviously-branded business accounts. The open-source community has produced a number of WhatsApp protocol re-implementations; the project uses one of the most widely deployed of these~\cite{baileys}.

\subsection{Anti-Spam and Rate-Limiting Heuristics}
A non-trivial part of the engineering effort for SIM-based WhatsApp automation goes into avoiding the spam-detection heuristics that Meta runs against suspicious accounts. The exact rules are not published, but the community has converged on a small number of practices that reduce the rate at which accounts are flagged. Mihail et al.~\cite{antispam} review the academic literature on spam detection in messaging platforms; the recurring themes are the importance of randomised intervals between messages, natural language variation to defeat hash-based fingerprinting, the simulation of typing presence and the enforcement of conservative daily caps. The platform implemented in this project addresses each of these signals.

\subsection{Account Aggregator Framework}
The Account Aggregator framework~\cite{aarbi}, regulated by the Reserve Bank of India, allows a regulated Financial Information User (FIU) to request bank statements and similar financial information with explicit, time-bound and revocable consent. The collections platform consumes AA-derived bank-statement data so that the AI calling conversation can branch on signals such as recent salary credit or balance trend, lifting the prompt from a generic ``please pay'' to a tailored ``we noticed your salary was credited on the 1st, what date works for you''.

\section{Problem Identification}
On the basis of the prior art reviewed above, the specific engineering problems this project sets out to solve can be sharpened as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Cycle-level case management.} Absorb the monthly delinquent-allocation CSV, validate it, deduplicate it, assign cases to agents according to the tenant's policy, and render them in a paginated dashboard.
\item \textbf{Comprehensive case detail.} Show every artefact relevant to a customer on a single page -- contact information, loan and payment history, prior call transcripts, WhatsApp and SMS threads, AA-derived signals -- with careful pagination and lazy loading.
\item \textbf{Bulk campaign execution.} Wrap a subset of cases as a single batch creatable from a CSV, schedulable, pausable and resumable, integrated with both the AI voice engine and the multi-channel messaging layer.
\item \textbf{Speed dialer.} Auto-load the next case the moment the agent submits a disposition.
\item \textbf{AI voice calling.} Trigger the AI voice agent from a campaign or as a manual-call fallback; parse the webhook, persist the outcome and advance the case through the workflow.
\item \textbf{WhatsApp automation.} Send from a pool of consumer-grade SIMs, rotate the pool, vary message bodies, throttle to stay below daily caps, and route around banned numbers without operator intervention.
\item \textbf{SMS automation.} Dispatch SMS through a real Android phone on a real consumer SIM, driven by a mobile app.
\item \textbf{Auditability.} Log every action so the QA team can reconstruct what happened to any given case at any given point in time.
\item \textbf{Multi-tenant isolation.} Serve multiple lender tenants from a single deployment with strict isolation of data, identity and configuration.
\end{enumerate}

These nine problems form the working set against which the methodology and implementation in the next chapter is judged.
