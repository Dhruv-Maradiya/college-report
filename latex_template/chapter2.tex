\chapter{Literature Review and Problem Identification}
\label{Chapter 2}

\section{Existing Collection-Management Products}
The space of commercial collection-management products in India is dominated by a small handful of vendors whose offerings predate the recent wave of conversational AI. CreditMantri, Spocto, Cred and a few internal tools built by the larger private-sector banks make up the majority of the visible deployments. The architecture of these products tends to follow a familiar shape -- a case management database, a dialer integration, some reporting -- but the actual conversational layer (the part that does the calling and the messaging) is almost always human-driven. WhatsApp, where it is used, is plugged in through the official Cloud API published by Meta and is therefore subject to template-approval delays, throughput throttling and per-message pricing that grows linearly with volume.

The academic literature on collection software per se is thin -- most of the deep work happens inside banks and is not published -- but the adjacent literature on customer-relationship-management systems, on contact-centre optimisation and on credit-scoring models is large. Verma, Mathur and Singh~\cite{vermaml} survey machine learning techniques applied to behavioural credit data, several of which are directly applicable to predicting which customer is most likely to respond to which channel. Wang and Zhang~\cite{contactcenter} review predictive dialers and the queue-management algorithms that underpin them. None of this prior work, however, addresses the combination of AI voice calling, AA-data-driven prioritisation and SIM-based WhatsApp automation that motivates the present project.

\section{Conversational Voice Agents}
The maturity of conversational voice agents is the single biggest technology shift that makes an AI-driven collection platform possible. The early generations of interactive voice response (IVR) -- the touch-tone menus that everyone has shouted at on the bank's customer-care line -- could not hold an open-ended conversation and were limited to deterministic decision trees. Modern voice agents are built on a pipeline of automatic speech recognition (ASR), a large language model (LLM) for dialogue generation, and a text-to-speech (TTS) synthesiser, with each component reaching real-time latency budgets that did not exist three years ago.

Bolna~\cite{bolna} is the conversational voice provider that Ignosis uses in production. Internally Bolna chains a low-latency ASR (typically Deepgram or a Whisper-based model), a fine-tuned LLM hosted on a serverless GPU backend and a TTS engine that can emit Indian English with regional-accent variation. The latency budget Bolna advertises is roughly 700~ms from the customer finishing their utterance to the agent beginning to speak, which is below the 800--900~ms threshold at which human listeners begin to detect awkwardness. The agent state and the prompt template are configured per tenant through a YAML file that the engineering team manages with a small Python tool.

Open-source baselines such as Pipecat~\cite{pipecat} and LiveKit Agents~\cite{livekit} were briefly evaluated by the engineering team before the choice of Bolna was finalised. The decision matrix turned out to be dominated by Indian-English ASR accuracy on rural and semi-urban customers, the ability to pass the customer's bank-statement-derived context into the prompt, and the maturity of the post-call extraction pipeline (Bolna emits a structured JSON of the outcome -- promise-to-pay, dispute, wrong-number, no-answer -- which is consumed by the workflow engine).

\section{The WhatsApp Multi-Device Protocol}
WhatsApp's multi-device protocol, rolled out in 2021, allows up to four secondary devices to share end-to-end-encrypted access to a primary phone's WhatsApp account without the phone needing to be online. The protocol uses a Signal-derived key ratchet for message encryption and a per-device session key for the secondary devices. The official integration path for businesses is the WhatsApp Cloud API, which sits on top of the same protocol but exposes a REST interface and enforces a template-message policy for the first message in any conversation. The Cloud API throttles at the conversation level and prices each message individually.

For a collection use case, the Cloud API is operationally restrictive in three ways. First, every outbound template has to be approved by Meta, which adds days to the iteration loop when new wording is required. Second, the per-message pricing makes the channel uneconomic for the long-tail of low-ticket-size cases, where the recovery itself may only be a few hundred rupees. Third, customers tend to be more responsive to messages that come from real consumer-grade WhatsApp numbers than from the obviously-branded business accounts.

The open-source community has produced a number of WhatsApp protocol re-implementations to support multi-device automation without going through the Cloud API. Baileys~\cite{baileys} is the most widely deployed of these: it is a TypeScript library that speaks the WhatsApp WebSocket protocol directly, supports pairing through QR codes and persists session state through a multi-file authentication store. whatsapp-web.js~\cite{wajs}, in contrast, drives a real Chromium browser through Puppeteer and screen-scrapes the WhatsApp Web client. Both libraries are gray-area in the sense that they reverse-engineer Meta's protocol; both have been deployed for years in collection-adjacent industries without legal action; both are sensitive to upstream protocol updates and require occasional patching.

\section{Anti-Spam and Rate-Limiting Heuristics}
A non-trivial part of the engineering effort for SIM-based WhatsApp automation goes into avoiding the spam-detection heuristics that Meta runs against suspicious accounts. The exact rules are not published, but the community has converged on a small number of practices that materially reduce the rate at which accounts are flagged or banned. Mihail et al.~\cite{antispam} review the academic literature on spam detection in messaging platforms and the corresponding evasion strategies. The recurring themes are the importance of randomised intervals between messages, the use of natural language variation to defeat hash-based fingerprinting, the simulation of typing presence and the enforcement of conservative daily caps.

Empirically the strongest signals that a number is being used for spam appear to be:
\begin{itemize}
\item A very high message rate from a freshly registered number.
\item Long sequences of identical message bodies.
\item Bursty sending without natural break patterns.
\item A high rejection rate from recipients (block, report).
\item A pattern of messages timed mechanically (e.g.\ exactly 30 seconds apart).
\end{itemize}

The platform built in this project addresses each of these signals: each sender phone has a daily cap, every outgoing message body is varied at the word level using inline alternates and zero-width-space insertion, the inter-message delay is jittered by $\pm$40 percent around a per-sender base interval, batches of messages are followed by short breaks that imitate human typing breaks, and the workers honour an active-hours window that maps to typical Indian working hours.

\section{Account Aggregator Framework}
The Account Aggregator framework~\cite{aarbi}, regulated by the Reserve Bank of India through Master Direction NBFC-AA dated 2016 and progressively expanded ever since, allows a regulated Financial Information User (FIU) to request the bank statements, GST returns or insurance information of a customer with their explicit, time-bound and revocable consent. Ignosis is one of the larger FIUs in the ecosystem. The collections platform consumes AA-derived bank-statement data through the same internal data layer that the AA / FIU product uses, which means that when a customer in the collections cycle has an active AA consent on file the collection workflow can branch on signals such as recent salary credit, recent large debits, balance trend or detected income source. This is the data input that lifts the AI calling conversation from a generic ``please pay'' to a tailored ``we noticed your salary was credited on the 1st, what date works for you''.

\section{Identity and Access Management with Keycloak}
Keycloak~\cite{keycloak} is the open-source identity and access management server used by Ignosis as the single sign-on backbone. Each tenant is implemented as a separate Keycloak realm, with its own users, groups, client scopes and role bindings. The Spring Boot backend integrates with Keycloak through the OAuth 2 Resource Server and the Keycloak admin client. Per-request tenant resolution is host-based: the URL the user hits determines the Keycloak realm that the JWT was issued by, and a small thread-local context object propagates the tenant identity through every downstream call, including across asynchronous boundaries. The use of Keycloak removes a large amount of authentication code that would otherwise have to be written by the engineering team, supports federation to lender-side identity systems (some banks insist on running their own IdP and federating into ours), and lets us move customer-facing collections agents and bank-side operations users behind the same access-control surface.

\section{Apache Solr for Case Search}
The case search layer is implemented over Apache Solr~\cite{solr}, a search server built on top of Apache Lucene. Each tenant has a dedicated Solr core into which the case data is indexed. Solr was chosen over Elasticsearch for two reasons: it was already in use elsewhere in the company for the AA / FIU product, and the eDisMax query parser is sufficient for the relatively simple lookup patterns that the collections dashboard requires (find by phone, by name, by loan account number, with faceted filtering by bucket, agent and status). The Solr client pool inside the Spring Boot backend is one client per tenant, with a custom Apache HTTP connection manager configured to prevent the idle-connection leaks that bit the team earlier in the year on the AWS ECS containers.

\section{Workflow Engines and Rule Evaluation}
The collection workflow is configurable per tenant -- a private bank tenant might prefer to call before sending a WhatsApp, while an NBFC tenant might prefer to send the WhatsApp first and only call if the message bounces. Hard-coding this would explode the conditional logic across the codebase, so the engineering team built a small MVEL2-based workflow engine inside the orchestrator. MVEL (the MVFLEX Expression Language)~\cite{mvel} is a Java-friendly expression language that evaluates string templates against a context object, in our case a map of workflow variables documented in a dedicated Markdown reference. Workflow nodes are defined declaratively, the transitions between them are MVEL expressions, and the engine drives the case through the graph in response to call outcomes, payment events and clock ticks. This is by far the most powerful single piece of infrastructure in the platform, and the project as a whole would not be feasible without it.

\section{Observability: Prometheus, Sentry and ClickHouse}
The observability layer of the platform combines three open-source tools. Prometheus~\cite{prom} scrapes Micrometer-emitted metrics from the Spring Boot actuator endpoint; the dashboard for those metrics is hosted on Grafana with role-based access. Sentry~\cite{sentry} (in our case the internal compatibility shim, not the public SaaS) captures exceptions and ties them to release tags. ClickHouse~\cite{clickhouse} is used as the analytics warehouse: every business-relevant event -- call disposition, escalation, prompt-variable miss, recovery -- is fire-and-forget inserted into a ClickHouse table that is queried both by the in-product dashboard and by the data team.

\section{Problem Identification}
On the basis of the prior art reviewed in the preceding sections, the specific engineering problems that this project sets out to solve can be sharpened as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Cycle-level case management.} Each tenant onboards its delinquent allocations as a monthly CSV. The platform must absorb that CSV, validate it, deduplicate it against the previous month's cases, assign cases to agents according to the tenant's allocation policy, and render the assigned cases in a paginated dashboard for the agent.
\item \textbf{Comprehensive case detail.} The agent needs to see, on a single page, every artefact relevant to a customer: contact information, demographic snapshot, full loan and payment history, every prior call transcript and outcome, every WhatsApp and SMS thread, every AA-derived data signal, and the current bucket. Pulling this together synchronously from a normalised database would be slow, so the layer needs careful pagination, lazy loading and selective denormalisation.
\item \textbf{Bulk campaign execution.} A campaign is a wrapper around a subset of cases that the manager wants to execute as a single batch. It must be creatable from a CSV (with validation against required columns), schedulable, pausable and resumable, and it must integrate with both the AI voice calling engine and the multi-channel messaging layer.
\item \textbf{Speed dialer.} When an agent is working through a campaign, the workflow must auto-load the next case as soon as the agent submits the disposition for the current call. There must be no waiting on the agent's part to click through to the next case.
\item \textbf{AI voice calling.} The AI voice agent must be triggerable both as part of a campaign and as a standalone fallback when an agent's manual call fails. The call outcome must be parsed from Bolna's webhook, persisted, and used to advance the case through the workflow.
\item \textbf{WhatsApp automation.} The platform must send WhatsApp messages from a pool of consumer-grade phone numbers, rotate the pool to spread load, vary the message body to defeat spam detection, throttle the sending rate to stay below the daily cap, and route around banned numbers without operator intervention.
\item \textbf{SMS automation.} For cases where WhatsApp delivery fails, the platform must send a SMS through a real Android phone on a real consumer SIM, with the dispatch driven by a mobile app running on the agent's or the manager's phone.
\item \textbf{Auditability.} Every action -- every call, every message, every state transition, every workflow node entry -- must be logged in a manner that lets the QA team reconstruct what happened to any given case at any given point in time.
\item \textbf{Multi-tenant isolation.} The platform must serve multiple lender tenants from a single deployment with strict isolation of data, of identity and of configuration.
\end{enumerate}

These nine problems form the working set against which the methodology and implementation in the next chapter is judged.
