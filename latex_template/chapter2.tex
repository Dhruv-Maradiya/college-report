\section{Previous Approaches to Solve the Problem}

\subsection{Existing Collection-Management Products}
The commercial collection-management space in India is dominated by a small set of vendors whose products predate the current wave of conversational AI. CreditMantri, Spocto and Cred make up most of the visible market, alongside a few internal tools that the larger private-sector banks have built for themselves. Architecturally these products look similar: a case database, a dialer integration, some reporting on top. What is missing is any meaningful conversational layer that is not human-driven. WhatsApp, when used at all, is plugged in through the official Cloud API from Meta, which means template approvals, throughput throttling and per-message pricing that scales linearly with volume. The academic literature on collection software is thin; most of the serious work happens inside banks and never gets published. The adjacent literature is larger. Verma, Mathur and Singh~\cite{vermaml} survey machine learning techniques on behavioural credit data, several of which apply directly to predicting which customer is most likely to respond on which channel. Wang and Zhang~\cite{contactcenter} review predictive dialers and the queue-management algorithms behind them. None of this prior work, however, looks at the combination of AI voice, AA-driven prioritisation and SIM-based WhatsApp automation that is the basis of this project.

\subsection{Conversational Voice Agents}
The biggest single technology shift behind this kind of platform is the maturity of conversational voice agents. A modern voice agent is a pipeline -- automatic speech recognition (ASR), a large language model (LLM) for the actual dialogue, and a text-to-speech (TTS) synthesiser -- and each piece is now fast enough to meet real-time latency budgets that did not exist three years ago. The voice stack Ignosis uses in production is the firm's own internal voice AI platform~\cite{bolna}: it chains a low-latency ASR, a fine-tuned LLM and a TTS engine that produces Indian English with regional-accent variation. Its target latency is around 700~ms from the customer finishing speaking to the agent beginning to reply, which sits comfortably below the 800--900~ms band at which human listeners start to feel something is off. Open-source baselines such as Pipecat~\cite{pipecat} and LiveKit Agents~\cite{livekit} were evaluated briefly before the internal platform was settled on. The decision came down to two things: how well the ASR handled rural and semi-urban customers in Indian English, and how mature the post-call extraction was. \subsection{The WhatsApp Multi-Device Protocol}
WhatsApp's multi-device protocol lets up to four secondary devices share end-to-end-encrypted access to a primary phone's account without that phone being online. The official integration path for businesses is the WhatsApp Cloud API, which exposes a REST interface and forces every first message in a conversation to be a pre-approved template. For collections, this is operationally restrictive in three ways. Every template must be approved by Meta, which can take days. The per-message price makes the channel uneconomic for low-ticket cases. And in practice customers respond much better to messages from real consumer-grade WhatsApp numbers than from obviously branded business accounts. To work around these, the open-source community has produced several reimplementations of the WhatsApp protocol; the project uses one of the most widely deployed of these~\cite{baileys}.

\subsection{Anti-Spam and Rate-Limiting Heuristics}
A real chunk of the engineering work on SIM-based WhatsApp goes into staying ahead of Meta's spam-detection heuristics. The exact rules are not published, but the open-source community has converged on a handful of practices that visibly reduce flagging. Mihail et al.~\cite{antispam} review the academic literature on spam detection in messaging platforms, and the same themes show up over and over: randomised intervals between messages, language-level variation to defeat hash-based fingerprinting, simulated typing presence, and conservative daily caps. The platform built in this project hits each of these.

\subsection{Account Aggregator Framework}
The Account Aggregator framework~\cite{aarbi}, regulated by the RBI, lets a registered Financial Information User (FIU) pull bank statements and similar financial information with explicit, time-bound, revocable consent from the customer. The collections platform consumes this data so that the AI calling script can branch on real signals -- a recent salary credit, a balance trend -- and turn a generic ``please pay'' prompt into a much more specific ``we noticed your salary credit came in on the 1st, what date works for you''.

\section{Problem Identification}
Reading the prior art against the gaps it leaves, the specific engineering problems this project sets out to solve are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Cycle-level case management.} Take the monthly delinquent-allocation CSV, validate it, deduplicate it, assign cases to agents per the tenant's policy, and render them in a paginated dashboard.
\item \textbf{Comprehensive case detail.} Bring every artefact relevant to a customer onto one screen -- contact info, loan and payment history, past call transcripts, WhatsApp and SMS threads, AA signals -- with sensible pagination and lazy loading so the page actually opens.
\item \textbf{Bulk campaign execution.} Bundle a set of cases as a single batch that can be created from a CSV, scheduled, paused and resumed, and that ties into both the AI voice engine and the multi-channel messaging layer.
\item \textbf{Speed dialer.} The moment the agent submits a disposition, the next case has to already be on the screen.
\item \textbf{AI voice calling.} Trigger the AI voice agent from a campaign, or manually as a fallback. Parse its callback, persist the outcome, and walk the workflow forward.
\item \textbf{WhatsApp automation.} Send from a pool of consumer SIMs, rotate them, vary the body of every message, throttle each sender to stay below its daily cap, and route around banned numbers without anyone needing to step in.
\item \textbf{SMS automation.} Dispatch SMS from a real Android phone on a real consumer SIM, driven by a mobile app.
\item \textbf{Auditability.} Log enough that QA can reconstruct what happened to any case at any point in time.
\item \textbf{Multi-tenant isolation.} Serve multiple lender tenants from one deployment with strict isolation of data, identity and configuration.
\end{enumerate}

These nine items are the working set the next chapter is judged against.
