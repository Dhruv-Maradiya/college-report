\chapter{Software Design}
\label{Chapter 4}

The previous chapter described the platform from the angle of the features that got built. This chapter steps back to the design level: what the design was being tested against, how the system decomposes into components, the data model that ties them together, the principal sequence flow, and the security model.

\section{Design Goals}

The platform was designed against a short, prioritised list of goals. Tenant isolation is at the top, because a cross-tenant data leak is the kind of thing you cannot walk back in a regulated environment. Configurable collection policy ranks above cost-aware channels for a simple reason: any tenant whose policy is hard-coded into the orchestrator will eventually need a code change just to be onboarded, and that does not scale. The remaining goals are operational -- auditability, observability and graceful degradation. The full list is in Table~\ref{tab:design-goals}.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Prioritised design goals.}
\label{tab:design-goals}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{l X}
\toprule
\textbf{Goal} & \textbf{Design implication} \\
\midrule
Tenant isolation by default & Per-tenant identity realm, per-tenant search partition, tenant-scoped database queries, thread-local tenant context propagated across asynchronous boundaries. \\
Configurable collection policy & Workflow stored as data (nodes plus boolean transitions), not as code. Tenant edits do not require a deployment. \\
Multi-channel orchestration & Channels are workflow action types. Adding a new channel reduces to adding a new node type, not rewriting the orchestrator. \\
Auditability & Every state transition writes a row to the case timeline; every outbound action carries an idempotency key. \\
Graceful degradation & Database-backed job queue, per-job retry policy with exponential backoff, dead-letter handling for permanent failures. \\
Cost-aware channels & SIM-based WhatsApp and SMS rather than vendor-priced APIs; AI calls only when the workflow asks for them. \\

\section{Data Model}

The data model is a normalised relational schema, with denormalised projections cached in a search index for queries and in an analytics event store for reporting. The most important relationship in the model is between the case and its per-cycle allocation. The case is a stable handle on the customer's relationship with the lender. The allocation is the cycle-specific projection the workflow engine actually drives. Splitting the two keeps the case timeline append-only across cycles, and lets a case carry its history forward even when its workflow position is reset at the start of a new cycle. The second important design choice is the append-only case-event table: every action the platform takes against a case appends a row, so timeline queries read from that table directly, while queries that need the current state read materialised columns on the case and the allocation. This separation keeps the live tables small and the timeline easy to audit.

\section{Principal Flow: AI Voice Call}

The AI voice call is the most architecturally instructive flow because it crosses every major component and exercises the workflow engine on both the outbound and the inbound side. The orchestrator's scheduler reaches an AI-call node for a case and schedules two jobs: a call-initiate job to run now, and a defensive status-poll job five minutes later. The executor pulls the next from-number, builds the request and hands it to the internal voice AI platform. The platform places the call through the telephony gateway and returns a terminal callback when the call ends. The callback processor persists the disposition, cancels the poll job, emits metrics, and the engine evaluates the outgoing transitions of the current node so the workflow advances on the same request. Two things here are worth calling out. The defensive poll job exists because the callback is best-effort: without a polling fallback, a dropped callback would leave the case parked indefinitely. And the cancellation is implemented as a soft cancel rather than a hard one. The poll job is allowed to run, sees the case already terminated, and exits. That is more robust against eventual consistency in the database than a hard cancel would be.

\section{Security Design}

Security sits on independent layers so that a breach of one does not compromise the layers below. Users authenticate against the identity layer with short-lived signed tokens. Every API call resolves the tenant identifier from the URL prefix and rejects any token whose realm does not match. A thread-local tenant context propagates the identity through every downstream call, including across asynchronous boundaries. Role-based access control is enforced both on the backend and on the dashboard. Every database query is scoped to the tenant, every search query is routed to the tenant's partition, every analytics query carries the tenant tag in its filter, and cross-tenant queries are simply not allowed at the data-access layer. Inbound callbacks are authenticated with a signed body digest, or with a monthly-rotated API key, and the secret lives in the cloud provider's secrets manager. A separate set of compliance controls -- call-frequency guards, do-not-call honour periods, PII access logging, recording retention -- is enforced uniformly across all tenants, no matter how an individual tenant has configured its workflow.
